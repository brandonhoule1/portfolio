THREE LEADERSHIP LENSES · PROPOSED APPROACH

Different responsibilities. The same respect for people and the work.

Each role changes where I would focus. I would learn what employees and leaders already rely on, preserve what is useful, and look for routine effort AI can take on—so people have more room for judgment, feeling, thinking, creativity, relationships, and decisions.

AI ENABLEMENT & DEVELOPER EXPERIENCE

Make the work easier to do well.

I would pay attention to how people experience everyday work and where routine coordination gets in the way of doing their best work.

  • Learn from employees about the friction they face and the practices, tools, and knowledge that already help them.
  • Build capability with the people doing the work, making room for learning, problem-solving, creativity, and collaboration.
  • Notice whether the change improves the work—and what people choose to do with the time and attention they regain.

QUALITY ENGINEERING LEADERSHIP

Give teams more room to find risk early.

Quality depends on the knowledge testers, developers, and product partners bring to the work. AI can help them consider more possibilities; it cannot establish confidence by itself.

  • Understand where teams have time to explore unusual conditions and where repetitive work narrows their attention.
  • Use AI to suggest questions and scenarios while people judge what matters, what the evidence supports, and whether a release is ready.
  • Look for opportunities to spend more effort preventing problems and learning from how software behaves in use.

Relevant experience: Led a five-person QA team, coordinated testing strategy, and advanced shift-left practices, automation, and release quality. See QA leadership experience.

AI SOLUTIONS & ENTERPRISE TECHNOLOGY

Improve what matters and own what follows.

I would start with the people and business need, taking existing systems and knowledge as assets to understand before deciding what should change.

  • Keep options open—including reuse, partnership, incubation, purchase, or deciding not to proceed—based on the need, constraints, and consequences.
  • Consider the full responsibility of a solution: delivery, support, reliability, incidents, and continued improvement after launch.
  • Learn from outcomes over time, including service and quality, operating costs, and where employees put reclaimed capacity.

What would tell me the change helped? I would compare the work before and after, then ask people what became possible with the time they regained. Time made available is added capacity; it is not automatically a financial saving.

GOVERNANCE STARTER KIT

Make responsibility clear before AI changes the work.

A practical starting checklist for teams considering an AI-enabled workflow. Scale the review to the consequences, the people affected, and the organization’s existing controls.

01 · DEFINE THE USE

Establish purpose and ownership

  • Name the business owner and the people accountable for the system.
  • Describe the work being changed, who is affected, and the intended benefit.
  • Identify the data involved, its source, permitted use, and sensitivity.
  • Check applicable organizational policy, contractual duties, and legal review needs.
02 · ASSESS THE CONSEQUENCES

Decide where human authority belongs

  • Consider what could happen if the output is wrong, incomplete, biased, or unavailable.
  • Set the system’s allowed role: inform, recommend, prepare, or act.
  • Specify which decisions require a person’s review and approval before action.
  • Define when the system must defer, stop, or route an issue to an accountable person.
03 · VALIDATE BEFORE USE

Test against real operating conditions

  • Use representative examples, including difficult and unusual cases.
  • Check accuracy, consistency, access controls, data handling, and traceability.
  • Record limitations, unresolved risks, required human checks, and approval owners.
  • Agree on success measures and conditions that would pause or stop the work.
04 · OPERATE AND REVISIT

Keep ownership active after launch

  • Assign responsibility for monitoring, support, incidents, and user concerns.
  • Keep records sufficient to understand important outputs and decisions.
  • Provide a workable fallback when the system is unavailable or unreliable.
  • Review the controls when the model, data, workflow, or consequences change.

A starting point for discussion—not legal advice or a substitute for organization-specific security, privacy, compliance, and risk review.

OTHER LEADERSHIP MODELS

Ways I frame the work.

Business-first opportunity model

Choose work because it matters to people and the organization, not because a tool is available.

Human–AI work design

Shape the relationship between automation and human judgment around the consequences of the work.

Adoption as operating-model change

Consider how responsibilities, skills, and decisions shift when AI becomes part of everyday work.

Evidence-to-value model

Distinguish activity from meaningful improvement, and make uncertainty visible in the story.

Build, buy, partner, or defer

Keep multiple paths open, including the choice to wait until the organization is ready.

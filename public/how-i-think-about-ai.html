HOW I THINK ABOUT AI · A LEADERSHIP PERSPECTIVE

AI for routine work. People for what makes us human.

I believe organizations should use AI to take on routine work. People bring the humanity: judgment, feeling, thinking, creativity, and decisions. They set direction, build relationships, and see possibilities the organization has not reached yet.

01 · FIND THE ROUTINE WORK

Put AI to work on routine effort.

I would begin by listening to the people doing the work and the leaders accountable for its outcomes. I want to understand where effort goes, what slows people down, what customers or colleagues experience, and which parts of the current work people value.

Repeated status chasing, entering the same information in several places, searching across disconnected systems, and coordinating predictable requests can consume attention without using much human judgment. I look for ways AI can take on that routine effort, while keeping people responsible for judgment, creativity, relationships, and consequential decisions.

The question I come back to is: if people had more time and attention, what valuable work could they do that is difficult to get to today? Their answers help define what a useful improvement would mean.

02 · RESPECT WHAT EXISTS

Build on what people have already made useful.

Current systems, practices, documentation, and informal knowledge carry the history of how an organization gets work done. Even a clumsy handoff may exist for a reason: a control, a customer need, a prior failure, or a responsibility that is not obvious from a process diagram.

I would learn what people rely on and why before proposing a replacement. Keep what still serves the work. Improve what creates avoidable friction. Connect or extend existing capabilities where that makes sense. Nothing useful should be discarded simply because a new technology has arrived.

The people who built and maintain today's environment are essential partners in shaping what comes next. Their experience is part of the context a new capability needs.

03 · UNDERSTAND THE BUILDING BLOCKS

Give each capability a role that fits.

Prompts, skills, agents, and workflows are different ways of bringing AI into work. I think about the responsibility each one carries, the knowledge it needs, and how a person can judge whether it helped.

Prompts

Express intent and relevant context. A clear request can help, while the answer still needs review against the real task and its consequences.

Skills and shared guidance

Make useful knowledge easier to reuse. People closest to the work should be able to improve it and understand where the guidance came from.

Agents

Take on bounded responsibilities when the work, authority, and limits are understood. Uncertainty and decisions with meaningful consequences call for human attention.

Workflows

Connect tasks, information, and handoffs around how work actually moves. Clear ownership matters across every transition.

Multi-agent systems, or “swarms”

Coordinate distinct capabilities only when they improve the outcome enough to justify added coordination, cost, and oversight. More agents do not automatically mean better work.

These concepts describe a leadership lens, not a recipe for assembling a particular system. The right choice depends on the work, available context, risk, and the people who remain accountable.

04 · CONNECT CONTEXT

Help work find the right knowledge and people.

Useful work depends on context. A team understands local responsibilities, exceptions, and customer needs. A department connects priorities across teams. The wider organization sets policies, shared commitments, and boundaries that apply across those groups.

I think about these levels as connected sources of context, with coordination helping work reach the team or person best equipped to handle it. Enterprise direction should be visible locally; local knowledge should inform broader decisions. One should not erase the other.

An intake experience can help someone describe a problem in ordinary language and give leaders enough context to understand its impact and ownership. People should not need to know the internal org chart or choose a technology before they can raise an improvement idea.

05 · DESIGN FOR ACCOUNTABILITY

Make human authority visible in the work.

Some work can be supported with information or a draft. Other work may be appropriate for a bounded action under clear conditions. As uncertainty, reach, or potential impact grows, the case for human review and explicit ownership grows with it.

Where a decision requires approval, the responsible person should have a clear place to see what is being proposed, why it reached them, and what decision is needed. They should be able to approve, decline, or edit it. Their judgment should guide what happens next; a review step should not turn into a rubber stamp.

People also need to know when a system is uncertain, what information it used, and how to respond when it is wrong, unavailable, or outside its limits. Data access, traceability, monitoring, fallback, and escalation are part of responsible operation, not details to leave for later.

06 · SELECT IMPROVEMENTS

Choose the problems that deserve attention.

A recurring annoyance is worth hearing, but the first idea is not always the best investment. I would learn who is affected, how often it happens, what it costs in time or service, where exceptions appear, and what people already do to work around it.

Then I would weigh the likely benefit against the real conditions: data and knowledge quality, access, reliability, risk, operating expense, organizational readiness, and who would own the result. Sometimes the right improvement is a clearer policy, a simpler process, better integration, or no change at all.

The goal is to relieve a meaningful constraint and learn responsibly. A compelling demonstration or a large volume of automatable work does not, by itself, establish that an idea should come first.

07 · BRING PEOPLE INTO THE CHANGE

Adoption is part of the design.

Employees should help shape how a new capability fits into their work. They can tell us where it adds value, where it creates confusion, and which responsibilities or relationships need to stay with a person. Leaders should be clear about the reason for the change and how decisions will be made as work evolves.

People need practical chances to learn, ask questions, raise problems, and improve shared guidance. Support should be easy to find. Concerns about accuracy, workload, customer impact, and changing responsibilities deserve direct attention.

Reclaimed time is an opportunity, not an outcome we can assume. I would ask the people doing the work where that capacity can help most: improving service, solving persistent problems, supporting colleagues, building quality, or doing deeper thinking.

08 · APPLY THE LENS TO THE ROLE

The role changes the questions I ask.

The same belief—AI for routine work, people for the human work—leads to different leadership questions depending on the mandate. These are the areas I would pay attention to in each role, shaped by the organization and its priorities.

AI enablement

Are people learning with work that matters to them? Can they find practical help and understand when to verify or ask for support? Where does the capacity gained from routine work go?

Quality engineering

Can AI help teams see more risks, edge cases, and failure paths while reducing routine coordination? Do people have the evidence and context to judge quality and release readiness?

Enterprise AI solutions

Which organizational friction deserves attention? How should context and ownership travel across teams? Who will support the solution, learn from its outcomes, and remain accountable after launch?

Across all three, the aim is to make important work easier for people to do and improve—not to introduce technology without a clear human or organizational benefit.

09 · LEARN FROM THE RESULTS

Measure the change, including its human value.

Before changing a workflow, I would want an agreed view of its current effort, quality, volume, reliability, and service outcomes. Afterward, compare the same measures and listen to how the experience changed for employees and customers.

A shared view of improvements could explain what changed, who benefits, how it performs, and what it costs to build and operate. Relevant costs may include model and token use, compute, licenses, support, maintenance, and human review. Usage is useful operating information; it does not prove that a business outcome improved.

Time made available to people is different from cash saved. I would look at how employees use that capacity and what it makes possible, then distinguish measured financial savings from service improvements, avoided work, or new opportunities. The organization should be able to see both the investment and the benefit without overstating either.

Technology should make useful work easier—and give people more room to do what people do best.

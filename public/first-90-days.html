LEADERSHIP PLAN · FIRST 90 DAYS

Understand first. Then improve what matters.

A practical starting point for an AI enablement, quality engineering, or enterprise technology leadership role. The priorities and pace would reflect the organization’s mandate, its people, and what is already working.

AI for routine work. People bring judgment, feeling, thinking, creativity, and decisions.

THE STARTING POINT

Learn the organization before changing it.

  • Understand the mandate, the outcomes leaders are responsible for, and the decisions this role is expected to own.
  • Listen to employees, technical teams, partners, and leaders. Learn what makes their work harder and what they do not want to lose.
  • Treat current systems, expertise, practices, and experiments as assets to understand. Keep and extend what still serves the work.
  • Look first for routine effort AI can take on. Keep people responsible for judgment, feeling, thinking, creativity, relationships, and consequential decisions.
  • Establish what is true today before describing improvement. Separate measured results from estimates, expectations, and opinions.
01—30

LISTEN AND UNDERSTAND

Build a grounded picture of the work.

  • Align with the executive sponsor on priorities, expected outcomes, constraints, decision rights, and what must remain stable.
  • Meet the people doing and receiving the work. Ask where time goes, what causes friction, what requires human judgment, and what they would do with more capacity.
  • Follow a representative set of workflows from intake to outcome. Notice handoffs, waiting, duplicate entry, rework, exceptions, and customer or employee impact.
  • Inventory the systems, data, knowledge, AI capabilities, vendors, controls, and active experiments already in use. Learn why current arrangements exist before proposing replacements.
  • Understand team strengths, open roles, workload, dependencies, existing quality practices, and how change is communicated and supported.
  • Agree on a small set of baseline measures suited to the work, such as effort, volume, cycle time, quality, rework, service, reliability, and cost. Record gaps where evidence is not available.
By the end of this phase
  • A shared view of the mandate, current capability, useful assets, and major constraints.
  • A short list of recurring problems grounded in employee and stakeholder experience, with baseline evidence or clearly stated evidence gaps.
31—60

FOCUS AND SHAPE

Choose a meaningful problem, not a technology demo.

  • Compare candidate improvements by how often the problem occurs, who it affects, the effort or service impact, and how much human attention it consumes.
  • Ask whether AI can take on the routine portion reliably. If the main problem is unclear ownership, a poor policy, or a broken process, address that directly.
  • Work with employees and accountable owners to describe the better experience and what people should be able to focus on when routine effort is reduced.
  • Check the practical conditions: information quality, access, integration, reliability, security, privacy, oversight, support, and the consequences of an error.
  • Make responsibility explicit: what AI may prepare or do, where a person reviews or decides, how uncertainty is surfaced, and what happens when the system cannot proceed.
  • Estimate the full operating cost—including model or token use, compute, licenses, integration, human review, maintenance, and support—and compare it with the expected benefit.
  • Agree on baseline measures, learning questions, safeguards, and conditions to continue, change, or stop before expanding the work.
By the end of this phase
  • A prioritized opportunity with a clear reason for its place in the queue.
  • An agreed scope, accountable owner, human responsibilities, evidence plan, and realistic view of costs and risks.
61—90

LEARN AND DECIDE

Review what changed and make the next decision.

  • Put the change in front of representative users and real operating conditions, including unusual cases and failure paths.
  • Keep review and escalation practical. Give people the information and authority they need to correct, decline, or stop work when warranted.
  • Compare results with the baseline using the measures agreed in advance. Pair quantitative evidence with employee and customer feedback.
  • Track quality, reliability, exceptions, incidents, support effort, and operating costs alongside usage.
  • Ask what people are doing with capacity released from routine work. Look for improvements in service, problem-solving, quality, collaboration, or deeper thinking; do not count reclaimed time as cash savings by default.
  • Share findings, limitations, and unresolved risks with the people affected and the leaders accountable for the outcome.
  • Decide whether to improve, expand, integrate, pause, or stop. Name the owner and support needed for whatever continues.
By the end of this phase
  • An evidence-based decision and an accountable owner for the next step.
  • A clear account of what improved, what did not, what it cost, and what people were able to do with the capacity returned to them.

THE ROLE CHANGES THE EMPHASIS

Same belief. Different leadership questions.

AI enablement

  • Where can AI remove routine effort from everyday work?
  • What access, guidance, and learning help employees use it with confidence?
  • How will teams reinvest time and attention in work that needs people?

Quality engineering

  • Where can AI broaden test ideas and reduce routine coordination?
  • How do existing quality practices and team expertise shape what to improve?
  • How will people retain responsibility for evidence, risk, and release decisions?

Enterprise AI solutions

  • How should improvement ideas find the right context, owner, and team?
  • Which capabilities can connect to existing systems and knowledge?
  • How will leaders see outcomes, human benefit, operating cost, and accountability?

A USEFUL DAY-90 OUTCOME

Clarity about what deserves to happen next.

  • Leaders and employees share an understanding of the work, what already works, and where the meaningful friction lies.
  • One or more improvement opportunities have been considered against human value, feasibility, risk, and full operating cost.
  • Any work underway has clear ownership, appropriate human authority, and evidence that people can inspect.
  • The organization can choose its next move based on what it learned—not on an assumption that every workflow should use AI or every experiment should scale.

This is an illustrative leadership approach, not a claim about a completed company-wide program. Priorities and timing would adapt to the organization and role.

HIRING MANAGER GUIDE · 4 SECTIONS

AI for routine work. People for the work only people can do.

How I would lead AI enablement across people, quality, enterprise delivery, and measurable value.

TL;DR

AI should take on routine work. People bring judgment, feeling, thinking, creativity, relationships, and decisions. Enablement should build on what works and make room for those human contributions.

This guide describes how I would think about an AI enablement leadership role. It is a proposed perspective for a new organization; my resume and case studies document the work I have already led.

01

PEOPLE AND CAPABILITY

Make the change useful to the people doing the work.

TL;DR

Learn what people rely on, where routine effort gets in the way, and what they could do with more time and attention.

Skip to quality and confidence ↓

I would begin by learning from employees and leaders: what work they rely on, where routine effort gets in the way, and what should be preserved. Existing skills, systems, and practices are organizational knowledge. They are the starting point for change, not obstacles to clear away.

  • Understand what people experience before proposing where AI belongs.
  • Shape adoption with the employees closest to the work; their knowledge should influence what changes and how it feels to use.
  • Ask what people could contribute if repetitive coordination and information handling took less of their attention.
  • Build practical confidence around both the useful role of AI and the limits people should recognize.

The aim is to make valuable work easier to do and give people more space to improve the organization.

02

QUALITY AND CONFIDENCE

Use AI to widen attention. Keep judgment with people.

TL;DR

AI can help teams examine more possibilities; people decide what matters and whether the evidence is strong enough to act on.

Skip to enterprise delivery ↓

Quality engineering is part of AI enablement because trust depends on what teams can learn about a system and the work around it. AI may help people consider more cases, explore information, or handle routine preparation. It cannot decide whether a result is meaningful or whether the evidence is strong enough to act on.

  • Build on testers’, developers’, and product partners’ knowledge of risk and expected behavior.
  • Use broader coverage to support exploration and prevention, while people decide which risks matter.
  • Keep ownership, review, and escalation clear when an AI-assisted result affects a consequential decision.
  • Treat generated output as a question or candidate for review until the responsible people establish that it is reliable enough for its intended use.

Relevant experience: I led a five-person QA team, coordinated testing strategy, and advanced shift-left practices, automation, and release quality. Those experiences inform this perspective; they do not imply that AI can replace engineering judgment. Read the quality engineering case study.

03

ENTERPRISE DELIVERY

Connect useful context without making people adapt to the org chart.

TL;DR

Make useful knowledge easier to reach across teams, while matching automation and authority to the work and its consequences.

Skip to learning and value ↓

People experience one organization, even when information and work are divided among teams and systems. I would look for ways to carry useful context across those boundaries so a request can reach the right knowledge and expertise without asking employees to understand every internal handoff.

  • Start with a real employee or business need, and learn what existing tools and information already serve it.
  • Consider how local team knowledge can connect to department and enterprise priorities while remaining understandable to the people who own it.
  • Choose the level of automation to fit the work, the quality of available context, and the consequences of a mistake.
  • Keep people accountable for consequential choices, with authority to question or change the direction when the situation calls for it.
  • Give employees a clear way to raise improvement opportunities and leaders a shared view of what changed and who it helped.

This is an organizational design perspective, not a prescribed technical architecture. The details should follow the people, constraints, and systems already in place.

04

LEARNING AND VALUE

Measure the work that changed—and what people can do next.

TL;DR

Compare outcomes and operating costs, then learn how people use capacity freed from routine work. Time reclaimed is not automatically cash saved.

Read the case studies →

I would want leaders and employees to see whether an improvement made work better, not just whether a tool was used. That means understanding a useful baseline, comparing it with what happens after a change, and making operating costs visible alongside the benefits.

  • Compare relevant measures before and after, such as effort, cycle time, quality, service, and reliability.
  • Include the cost to build and operate the capability, including AI and infrastructure usage where it is material.
  • Ask people how they use time and attention that routine work no longer consumes—and what valuable work becomes possible.
  • Distinguish capacity made available from cash savings; time reclaimed does not automatically become a budget reduction.
  • Use what employees and customers experience to decide whether to continue, adjust, or stop an improvement.

The result I would look for: less avoidable friction, clearer accountability, and more opportunity for people to contribute judgment, creativity, care, and ideas where they matter.
